A ledger, a ship, a daughter who won’t stop counting.

For thirty-one years Krishnan Nair has written down the tides of Kochi harbour in a green clothbound ledger, by hand, to the minute.1 When he dies in the first week of the 1961 monsoon, his nineteen-year-old daughter Meenakshi walks his umbrella to the harbour office and finds a pencilled line beneath his last entry: the name of a rice boat, and the words third time.

Nobody has asked her to take over the tides. Nobody stops her either. As she learns to read the water on the north jetty, she learns to read the men who use it: the harbour master’s careful assistant, a timber merchant with two sets of books, a ship that sails heavier than rice should make her.2

The Tide Clerk is a novel about inheritance in its smallest, most stubborn form: the habit of paying attention. It is also, Priya Nair says, “a love letter to everyone whose job was to write things down, and who did it properly.”3

  1. 1The ledgers are real in spirit: Priya’s grandfather recorded rainfall in Ernakulam for most of his life, in pencil, in school exercise books.
  2. 2Seven years in the writing, and three drafts in which the ship had a different name.
  3. 3From her conversation with the Kochi Literary Circle, spring this year.
